Why this matters & what "needs" means

This piece is about getting really clear about what you need from your career — which is not the same as what you want. The smaller you can make your list of needs, the less restrictive they are and the more freedom you'll have to reinvent your career.

"Those who are the richest are those who need the least."

The goal is to be realistic about your true needs so you can create more space, energy and options.

Think of needs as the extrinsic rewards of your work and the fixed points that limit your flexibility: the income you need, work location and commute, hours you're available, acceptable work patterns (shifts? nights?), physical demands you can cope with, time away from home, and the perks that are absolutely essential. Being vague about money keeps people stuck, so it's worth looking at in detail.

Step 1 · first passInitial sense of your needs

Capture anything relevant and important to get an initial sense. Don't overthink it yet.

Needs or wants? (read before refining)

The fewer needs or limiting factors you have, the more flexibility you create. Take a hard look and make sure you're not making assumptions or restricting yourself unnecessarily. Don't assume you know what your partner, parents or children want — people can be surprisingly flexible and supportive when they know a change will make you happier and healthier.

Revisit your list and ask whether each item is really essential, or a "nice to have" you could live without, especially for a fixed period to help you get where you want to be.

Be careful not to give up what you want most for what you want now.

Step 2 · the money storyWhat income do you really need?

"Can't afford it" is one of the easiest excuses for not reinventing a career. Get into the actual numbers — there's no shortcut.

A great career lets you be truly you and use your unique strengths. If what you believe you can do is determined mainly by the size of your paycheck, you can end up paying a high price in countless other ways.
More prompts on spending & your "money story"

When we're unhappy or stressed at work we're tempted to spend to compensate — taxis because we're tired or late, treats, eating out because there's no time to shop, gym memberships we don't use, daily caffeine and alcohol to keep going. It adds up.

  • Is your current job "high maintenance"? How much goes on clothes, meals out and entertainment because it's part of the deal? Would that decline if you changed careers?
  • Vacations — if you were happier and more fulfilled at work, would you need to spend the same on these?
  • Commute — could it be cheaper, a smaller or more economical vehicle, or shared lifts?

Becoming aware of spending habits can create powerful change and let you rebuild life more economically — more freedom to live and work the way you want. If you need to, take time to cut costs or build a reinvention fund, and be realistic about the timescale. The time will pass anyway; the sooner you start, the sooner you get there.
